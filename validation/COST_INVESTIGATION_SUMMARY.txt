================================================================================
DRO-MES COMPUTATIONAL COST INVESTIGATION: SUMMARY
================================================================================
Prompted by the Phase 1-4 joint-RTG investigation's conclusion (see
validation/PHASE1-4_SUMMARY.txt): joint RTG's actual motivation was
information-theoretic soundness, not cost, and it turned out to be MORE
expensive per rollout step than the existing per-step reward (extra
Thompson sampling + GP fantasy-conditioning), not less. This investigation
asks a different, orthogonal question: if the actual goal is reducing
DRO-MES's computational cost, what are the real opportunities, and do they
cost anything in final performance?

Benchmark used throughout: Hartmann_6D, use_mes_reward=True,
rtg_schema="floored" (the current production default -- NOT "joint"),
gp_num_models=5, rollouts_per_iter=75, rollout_length=4, bo_iterations=30,
initial_points=5, dt_hidden=128/dt_layers=4/dt_heads=4. Chosen because
roi_candidates stays large throughout (~990-1000 points at every stage per
earlier Phase 2 v4 data), making any cost-savings signal maximally visible.


================================================================================
PART 1: MES REWARD COMPUTATION (compute_mes_reward, mes_reward.py)
================================================================================

--- Proposed alternatives (before implementing anything) ---
Six alternatives were proposed for reducing MES reward's cost:
  1. Replace the brentq/product-CDF Gumbel fit with Thompson sampling + MLE
     fit (reusing gumbel_thompson.py, already built and validated in
     Phase 1 of the joint-RTG investigation).
  2. Decouple the reward's candidate-set size from the acquisition's
     (subsample roi_candidates for the reward specifically).
  3. Cache the Gumbel(a,b) parameters across rollout steps instead of
     refitting every step.
  4. Vectorize compute_mes_reward's two separate .posterior() calls
     (roi_candidates, x_tau) into one batched call.
  5. Reduce mes_k (MC sample count for Term 2), currently 10.
  6. Reduce rollouts_per_iter or rollout_length for MES variants.
Alternatives #1 and #4 were selected as the most promising (cheap AND fixes
a real accuracy bug found in Phase 1; free engineering win, respectively)
and implemented first.

--- Implementation: solution #1 ---
compute_mes_reward's y* sampling changed from gumbel_sample_y_star
(product-CDF Gumbel fit via _bracket_for_quantiles + two brentq root-finds,
each evaluating an O(N_roi) product-of-CDFs ~30-50 times) to Thompson
sampling (one batched posterior.rsample() call, then max). Also fixes a
real accuracy problem: gumbel_sample_y_star uses the same independence
assumption Phase 1 of the joint-RTG investigation found to be a poor fit
under RBF-kernel correlation (KS 0.16-0.86); Thompson sampling removes that
assumption (Phase 1 re-run: KS 0.03-0.10).
gumbel_sample_y_star, _cdf_of_max, _bracket_for_quantiles, and the private
constants are left unchanged in mes_reward.py (still used by
validation/phase1_gumbel_quality.py's own audit, and available for anyone
wanting the old method) -- only compute_mes_reward's internals changed.

--- Implementation: solution #4 ---
The two separate .posterior() calls (roi_candidates, then x_tau) merged
into one .posterior() call over their concatenation. A GP's marginal
mean/variance at any point is unaffected by what other points are queried
alongside it, so this is a pure efficiency change, not an approximation.

--- CRITICAL FINDING: solution #1 alone made things WORSE at realistic scale ---
A direct scaling comparison (compute_mes_reward_baseline vs the new
implementation, varying N_roi) found a crossover:
    N_roi     baseline    fast (#1+#4)   ratio (fast/baseline)
      50       1.04ms        0.54ms          0.52x (faster)
     200       1.48ms        0.98ms          0.66x (faster)
     500       2.21ms        1.82ms          0.82x (faster)
    1000       3.80ms        5.16ms          1.36x (SLOWER)
Root cause: the original product-CDF method only ever touches MARGINAL
means/variances -- O(N_roi) per CDF evaluation, no covariance matrix --
which is exactly the independence assumption that makes it inaccurate, but
also what makes it cheap. rsample() needs the FULL joint covariance over
roi_candidates (a Cholesky factorization that scales worse than linearly),
so Thompson sampling is only cheaper up to roughly N_roi~500-700. Since
roi_candidates commonly reaches ~1000 in this codebase (and Hartmann_6D was
deliberately chosen to make this visible), solution #1 as originally
implemented would have made the full pipeline SLOWER, not faster.

--- Fix: roi_subsample ---
Added a roi_subsample parameter (default 150) to compute_mes_reward: when
roi_candidates exceeds this, a random subsample is used for the
rsample()-based y* estimation only (mu_x/sigma_x for term1 are unaffected,
since they only depend on x_tau). This keeps the rsample() call in the
cheaper regime regardless of the real roi_candidates size. Re-verified the
scaling comparison after the fix:
    N_roi     baseline    fast (subsampled)   ratio
      50       1.05ms        0.55ms              0.52x
     200       1.56ms        0.82ms              0.52x
     500       2.27ms        0.75ms              0.33x
    1000       3.92ms        0.79ms              0.20x (5x faster)
The fast implementation's cost is now roughly flat regardless of N_roi
(capped by roi_subsample), while baseline keeps growing.

--- Full before/after result (3 seeds, 30 BO iterations, 75 rollouts/iter) ---
                          Wall-clock (s)      MES-reward time (s)   Final regret
DRO-MES-Baseline          329.0 +/- 0.8           27.75 +/- 0.12     2.3185 +/- 0.0801
DRO-MES-Fast (#1+#4+sub)  305.6 +/- 0.4            6.92 +/- 0.00     2.2970 +/- 0.0694

Per-seed (baseline / fast):
  seed 42: wall_clock 328.4s / 306.1s   mes_reward_time 27.66s / 6.93s   final_regret 2.4511 / 2.3867
  seed 43: wall_clock 330.9s / 306.0s   mes_reward_time 28.03s / 6.91s   final_regret 2.1271 / 2.1271
  seed 44: wall_clock 327.7s / 304.7s   mes_reward_time 27.55s / 6.91s   final_regret 2.3772 / 2.3772

MES-reward-time speedup: 4.01x (27.75s -> 6.92s).
Overall wall-clock speedup: 1.08x (329.0s -> 305.6s) -- modest, because MES
reward computation was only ~8.4% of total wall-clock time even at
Hartmann_6D's large roi_candidates.
Final regret: statistically unchanged (2.3185 -> 2.2970, well within the
+/-0.07-0.08 seed noise) -- the optimization is accuracy-neutral, as
expected (confirmed, not just assumed, from Phase 1's KS-test validation).

CONCLUSION: solutions #1+#4+roi_subsample are a clean win. Live in
production mes_reward.py; no changes needed in dro.py (compute_mes_reward's
signature and both call sites are unchanged). Script:
mes_cost_benchmark.py. Full results: results/mes_cost_benchmark.json.


================================================================================
PART 2: WHERE DOES THE REMAINING ~92% OF WALL-CLOCK TIME GO?
================================================================================
Profiled one full 30-iteration, 75-rollout/iteration run (DRO-MES-Fast
implementation, single seed=42) by wrapping the sequential top-level phases
of the per-iteration loop with timers.

Total wall-clock: 310.5s

--- Top-level breakdown (sequential, sums to ~total) ---
  _update_models (GP fitting)                        5.1s   ( 1.6%)   31 calls
  _simulate_trajectory (rollout simulation)         129.9s   (41.8%)  2250 calls
  _train_decision_transformer (DT training)         175.3s   (56.4%)    30 calls
  residual (DT inference for real candidate,
            objective eval, logging)                  0.3s   ( 0.1%)

--- Nested breakdown WITHIN rollout-simulation time (overlaps, not additive) ---
  _optimize_acquisition       117.9s   (90.8% of rollout-sim, 38.0% of total)   9000 calls
  compute_mes_reward             7.1s   ( 5.5% of rollout-sim,  2.3% of total)  9000 calls
  residual (posterior.sample(), _extract_state, etc.)   4.9s  (3.8% of rollout-sim)

KEY FINDING: DT training (56.4%) and _optimize_acquisition inside rollouts
(38.0%) together account for 94.4% of total wall-clock. MES reward, even
before optimization, was only ever 8.4%; after Part 1's fix, 2.3%.

IMPORTANT ARCHITECTURAL FACT (verified by grepping all call sites):
_optimize_acquisition is called ONLY from within simulated rollouts
(_simulate_trajectory / _simulate_trajectory_joint) -- never for the real
candidate each BO iteration, which comes directly from the trained Decision
Transformer's forward pass. So any change to _optimize_acquisition's cost
only affects simulated-rollout fidelity / DT training data quality, never
a real-time decision directly.

Script: profile_dro_iteration.py. Raw log: this was a diagnostic run, not
saved to a persistent JSON (see /tmp/profile_dro_run.log if still present;
otherwise re-run the script to reproduce -- it is read-only/non-invasive).

PART 3: DT TRAINING EPOCHS -- ABANDONED (killed mid-run, no usable data)
================================================================================
A combined experiment (dt_acq_cost_benchmark.py) was designed to test BOTH
reducing DT training epochs (100 -> 50) AND the rollout-acquisition budget
(see Part 4) together, plus each individually, against the DRO-MES-Fast
baseline. Before that, a loss-curve diagnostic was run: one representative
75-rollout batch, DT trained for 100 "epochs" (100 separate 1-epoch calls
to _train_decision_transformer on the same batch, tracking loss each time,
since self.decision_transformer and self.optimizer persist across calls
and this is a faithful reproduction of what one real 100-epoch call does).

Loss curve (selected epochs):
  epoch   1: L_loc=0.001612
  epoch  16: L_loc=0.001336
  epoch  36: L_loc=0.001226
  epoch  56: L_loc=0.001205
  epoch  76: L_loc=0.001144
  epoch  96: L_loc=0.001106
  epoch 100: L_loc=0.001103   (min loss 0.001023 at epoch 99)

FINDING: loss does NOT plateau early -- it is still improving (slowly,
noisily) all the way to epoch 100. Cutting epochs to 50 would leave real
loss reduction on the table (epoch 50 loss ~0.0013 vs epoch 100's ~0.0011,
roughly 15-20% higher), an unquantified but real accuracy/speed tradeoff.

The full 9-run experiment (DT-Epochs50, RolloutAcq-Reduced, and Combined,
each x 3 seeds) was launched but ran far longer than estimated (~55+
minutes of CPU time without a single completed run) and was killed by
explicit user request before producing any usable data. Root cause of the
slowdown was not diagnosed (possible causes: background system load,
thermal throttling, or the DT-epochs cost not scaling as linearly with
epoch count as assumed -- not confirmed either way).

STATUS: DT training epoch reduction was never empirically tested. Given
the loss-curve diagnostic already shows a real, unquantified cost to
cutting epochs, and given the profiling in Part 2 shows this is the single
largest cost bucket (56.4%), this remains the most promising unexplored
lever if further wall-clock reduction is wanted -- but requires a properly
scoped, monitored experiment (single seed first, unbuffered output) before
drawing conclusions.


================================================================================
PART 4: ROLLOUT-ACQUISITION BUDGET REDUCTION -- TESTED, NOT ADOPTED
================================================================================
_optimize_acquisition (called only within simulated rollouts, see Part 2)
was made to accept a for_rollout: bool parameter (default False, fully
backward-compatible). When True, it reads separate rollout_opt_samples /
rollout_opt_restarts / rollout_refinement_samples config keys (each falls
back to the standard key's value if unset), instead of the standard
opt_samples/opt_restarts/refinement_samples used for the (never-invoked-
for-rollouts) real-candidate path. _simulate_trajectory's two call sites
were updated to pass for_rollout=True; _simulate_trajectory_joint's call
site was left unchanged (out of scope -- rtg_schema="joint" is not the
production default per the Phase 1-4 conclusion).

--- Per-call timing diagnostic (varying acquisition budget, fixed GP) ---
  opt_samples=1000 opt_restarts=10 refine_samples=100 (current)        -> 12.68ms/call
  opt_samples=500  opt_restarts=10 refine_samples=100 (half broad only) -> 12.12ms/call (barely changed)
  opt_samples=250  opt_restarts=5  refine_samples=50  (moderate cut)    ->  6.59ms/call (~1.9x faster)
  opt_samples=100  opt_restarts=3  refine_samples=30  (aggressive cut)  ->  3.93ms/call (~3.2x faster)
FINDING: halving broad-search samples alone barely helps -- the REFINEMENT
loop (num_restarts separate small-batch GP calls), not the broad search,
dominates _optimize_acquisition's cost.

--- Single-seed (seed=42) real-run result, Hartmann_6D, 30 iterations ---
                          Wall-clock    Speedup   Final regret   Delta vs baseline
DRO-MES-Fast (baseline)    306.1s        1.00x      2.3867            --
RolloutAcq-Moderate        248.0s        1.23x      2.7347          +0.348 (worse)
RolloutAcq-Aggressive      229.9s        1.33x      2.8497          +0.463 (worse)

Wall-clock speedups (1.23x/1.33x) are much smaller than the per-call
numbers (1.9x/3.2x) suggested, consistent with _optimize_acquisition being
only ~38% of total time (same pattern seen with MES reward in Part 1).
Both variants showed WORSE regret than baseline at this one seed, with
Aggressive worse than Moderate -- a plausible, monotonic fidelity/speed
tradeoff, though unconfirmed at N=1 (final_regret has shown +/-0.07-0.8
seed noise in every multi-seed experiment in this investigation).

DECISION (explicit user call, given the single-seed regret cost and modest
speedup): do not adopt. Full 3-seed confirmation was not run.

STATUS: for_rollout parameter and rollout_opt_samples/rollout_opt_restarts/
rollout_refinement_samples config keys remain in src/policy/dro.py as
working, backward-compatible machinery (no-op unless explicitly
configured -- no production config sets them). Easy to revisit with a full
3-seed run or a milder cut if there's reason to later.
Script: rollout_acq_cost_benchmark.py (has a --seeds CLI flag for partial
runs; a summary-printing bug for the single-seed/N=1 case was found and
fixed after this run -- future single-seed runs will print cleanly).

Of the ~92% of wall-clock time NOT spent on MES reward, DT training (56.4%
of total) and rollout-scoped acquisition optimization (38.0% of total) are
the only two levers identified. Only one (MES reward itself) has produced
a confirmed, free win so far. The rollout-acquisition lever was tested and
explicitly rejected due to an apparent (if unconfirmed) regret cost. DT
training epoch reduction remains the single largest unexplored opportunity,
but the loss-curve diagnostic already suggests it will not be free either.
