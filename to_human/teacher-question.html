MF-DRO · teacher investigation

Why the perfect teacher failed

The method learns from trajectories generated by a teacher. Giving it a perfect teacher — routes walking straight to the true optimum — made it dramatically worse. Twenty-one experiments later, the reason is not what any of my first three explanations said it was.

Borehole 8D and Hartmann 6D · n=5 seeds per arm · no p-values at this sample size · frozen metric: relative regret at cost 200

The settled answersettled

Teacher quality is irrelevant, and so is the variety of where the teacher goes. This is no longer inferred from comparing a few arms — it was swept directly on a single axis, with the manipulation verified before the outcome was read.

Walking toward a point already known to be good teaches almost nothing about where the optimum is — regardless of how good that point is, and regardless of whether every route heads somewhere different.

What actually separates the teachers

Two properties were varied independently: whether the teacher re-decides at every step, and whether it uses the method's own selection rule. Three of the four combinations work.

Freezing the good teacher costs about three and a half points and no improvements. Swapping the rule costs nothing. Only doing both at once — a fixed route toward somewhere chosen without consulting the model — costs twenty-eight points and every single run.

The learner does not copy the rule — it copies an averagenew

Every explanation that failed on this question changed how the teacher decides. This measures whether that choice ever reaches the learner's output at all.

Take the first point the learner picks on its own, and compare it across teachers on the same starting conditions. Swapping the teacher's decision rule barely moves that point. Replacing the teacher with one that picks at random moves it ten times further.

Borehole, five seeds, matched pair by pair. For scale: two runs of the same teacher that differ only in random seed sit 0.82 apart — far further than any rule change here.

The check that makes this a finding rather than an empty result: the random teacher moves the point 9.9× further than the rule swap does, the two sets of measurements do not overlap at all, and the random teacher's point lands nearer the middle of the box — exactly where a learner predicting an average would put it. The measurement can see teacher effects. It simply does not see the rule.

This is why six explanations failed. Every one of them changed the rule. And it is why the two changes that did work — selecting good trajectories, and shortening them — work for a different reason: neither changes the rule, both change what is being averaged.

It repeats on the second benchmark, which is the cleaner test

On Hartmann the same split appears: rule changes sit at 0.035–0.052 and perform at 7.9–10.6 against the control's 8.0; the averaging changes sit at 0.23–0.53 and degrade to 25–65. Hartmann is the better of the two tests because its failing arms take four distinct values, whereas on Borehole four of them are pinned at the same 43.94 floor — so nothing there can be read as a trend.

What this does not establish

Only two genuinely different rules were tested on Borehole and one on Hartmann, and all of them belong to the same family — they each chase a high predicted value with some allowance for uncertainty. A competent teacher built on a different principle is untested, and is the most likely way this result breaks. Two of the arms in the table above were first counted as rule changes and are not: they alter the learner's target, not the teacher's rule, and have been moved out of that column.

Three explanations, retractedretracted

Each of these was reported before it was tested properly. Each is withdrawn, with the experiment that killed it.

“The method's own rule is what the learner needs”

retracted by the UCB teacher — 15.13 against the control's 15.82, improving 5/5

A completely different, standard selection rule matches the control on regret, on improvement rate, and on the training signal. It is not the rule.

“The failing teachers generate too weak a signal to aim at”

retracted by the frozen control — signal 0.32, indistinguishable from the failing teachers, and it works anyway

This was my leading account for four rounds. The frozen control generates a signal as weak as the failing teachers and performs like the working ones. A weak signal does not cause the failure. The measurement behind the account was right; the causal story was not.

“It's about whether the teacher can adapt”

retracted by measurement — freezing costs ~9% of the signal; the failing teachers lose ~70%

This one was itself an over-correction: I found a genuinely uncontrolled variable and promoted it to the explanation without checking whether it was large enough to do the work. It was not.

The current accountcausal test pending

What the three working teachers share is that they query places the model itself picked — including the frozen one, whose fixed route was recorded from the model earlier. The three failing teachers query places chosen by something outside the model: the true optimum, a draw from the true objective, or nothing at all.

An action chosen by something the learner cannot observe cannot be learned from what it observes. The prediction is that the network gives up and predicts the average — and that is visible in its own behaviour.

The sharpest version: the random teacher is the most spread-out teacher of all — and it produces the most collapsed learner of all. A network imitating its teacher cannot do that. A network giving up and predicting the average does exactly that.

This also resolves something that sat unexplained for weeks. The network fits the failing teachers better — lower training loss — while emitting worse points. Low loss on clustered targets means it is predicting their average, not learning a mapping.

Since this was written — and now settled

The reasoning above was wrong in an instructive way. It assumed the learner cannot fit the failing teachers. It fits them fine — two and a half to four times better than the best possible constant guess, on all three. The failure is not in the learning.

What replaced it. The learner is only ever asked for a decision at the very first step of a route, and at that first step the situations it trained on are nearly identical to one another. So it can only reproduce its teacher's average first move. Teachers whose first move is chosen by consulting the model hand it a useful point. Teachers whose first move is a random draw hand it the average of a random draw — the middle of the search space, and useless.

And this was then tested by changing something, not by fitting more numbers. Two teachers were built: one consults the model only on its first step and moves at random for the other seven; the other does the reverse. The second follows the model on seven steps out of eight and is the better teacher by any conventional measure.

What this settles, and what it costs

A bad first step is fatal, whatever the rest of the route does. The seven-step teacher fails completely on both benchmarks tested — 43.94 against a working 15.82 on one, 46.45 against 7.99 on the other — and its learner queries the dead centre of the search space in both. That is the part that replicates.

Whether the other seven steps matter at all depends on the problem, and I got this wrong at first. On the first benchmark the one-step teacher matched the method itself (16.96 against 15.82), so those seven steps contributed essentially nothing — they affected only the bill, taking 1.82× the wall-clock side by side on the same machine for a worse result. On the second benchmark the one-step teacher is worse than the method on every seed (25.16 against 7.99). There, the later steps do real work.

I reported the stronger claim — "only the first step matters" — after four of five runs had finished on the second benchmark. The fifth reversed it. The claim that survives on both is the weaker one above.

That still explains the original puzzle. How good a route is, and how varied, both describe where the route goes — while the first step is what the learner is actually asked to reproduce. A perfect route's first step is a random starting point, whose average is the middle of the space, and that is exactly where its learner ends up querying.

Still open. The learner's query lands near its teacher's average first move, not exactly on it — about five standard errors away, on both benchmarks. Something else is also operating, and it has not been identified.

What this is worth in practicemeasured

If only the first step of a route reaches the learner, the rest is cost. On the first benchmark it can simply be removed.

Borehole, five seeds each. Every configuration improves on its starting design in all five runs.

The project's best result survives the cut, at about a fifth of the time. That is the practical payoff: the same answer for a fraction of the compute.

Two honest limits. The apparent improvement from shortening is not real at five seeds — the run-to-run spread is larger than the difference. What the data supports is "no worse, much faster", and I initially wrote "better" before checking.

And shortening costs consistency. The best setting at full length is by far the steadiest configuration measured, varying by 0.41 across seeds against 2.79 when shortened. If predictability matters more than wall-clock, the long version is still the one to use — and nothing in the average regret shows that.

Honest limits

The dispersion evidence is behavioural, measured at five seeds per arm. The same class of evidence has already produced one published finding that was later retracted, and one registered prediction that came out backwards. It is a consistent correlate across two benchmarks and seven teachers — not a demonstrated cause.

A precision claim made earlier here has been withdrawn: a standalone reconstruction of the training signal was reported as matching the measured values to within 8%, but it is itself noisy to about 6%, so that agreement was barely distinguishable from noise. It reproduces the separation between working and failing teachers — a factor of three — reliably. It does not reproduce the individual values, and on the one case it had never been checked against, it was wrong by a factor of nearly three.
