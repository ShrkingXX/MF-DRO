MF-DRO · teacher investigation

Why the perfect teacher failed

The method learns from trajectories generated by a teacher. Giving it a perfect teacher — routes walking straight to the true optimum — made it dramatically worse. Forty experiments later the reason is identified, and it is not what any of my six explanations said it was: the learner never learns a rule at all. It memorises one point per step, and only the first one is ever used.

Walking toward a point already known to be good teaches almost nothing about where the optimum is — regardless of how good that point is, and regardless of whether every route heads somewhere different.

Two properties were varied independently: whether the teacher re-decides at every step, and whether it uses the method's own selection rule. Three of the four combinations work.

The learner does not copy the rule — it copies an averagenew

Every explanation that failed on this question changed how the teacher decides. This measures whether that choice ever reaches the learner's output at all.

Take the first point the learner picks on its own, and compare it across teachers on the same starting conditions. Swapping the teacher's decision rule barely moves that point. Replacing the teacher with one that picks at random moves it ten times further.

The check that makes this a finding rather than an empty result: the random teacher moves the point 9.9× further than the rule swap does, the two sets of measurements do not overlap at all, and the random teacher's point lands nearer the middle of the box — exactly where a learner predicting an average would put it. The measurement can see teacher effects. It simply does not see the rule.

This is why six explanations failed. Every one of them changed the rule. And it is why the two changes that did work — selecting good trajectories, and shortening them — work for a different reason: neither changes the rule, both change what is being averaged.

It repeats on the second benchmark, which is the cleaner test

On Hartmann the same split appears: rule changes sit at 0.035–0.052 and perform at 7.9–10.6 against the control's 8.0; the averaging changes sit at 0.23–0.53 and degrade to 25–65. Hartmann is the better of the two tests because its failing arms take four distinct values, whereas on Borehole four of them are pinned at the same 43.94 floor — so nothing there can be read as a trend.

What this does not establish

Only two genuinely different rules were tested on Borehole and one on Hartmann, and all of them belong to the same family — they each chase a high predicted value with some allowance for uncertainty. A competent teacher built on a different principle is untested, and is the most likely way this result breaks. Two of the arms in the table above were first counted as rule changes and are not: they alter the learner's target, not the teacher's rule, and have been moved out of that column.

What failure actually looks like: walking into the middlenew

Until now “failing” meant a number that stayed high. Here is what the failing runs are physically doing: they walk into the middle of the search space and stay there.

Measured across 28 variants — different teachers, different constraints, different rollout lengths, different loss functions — how far a run's queries sit from the middle of the box tracks its final result almost perfectly (rank correlation −0.967). The best variant sits furthest out; the worst sits nearly dead centre.

These are opposite directions, not degrees of the same thing. A failing run is not a working run that improved less; it is a run travelling the other way.

The clinching comparison

Two variants were built to separate “the first step” from “the other seven”, and they invert perfectly. The first-step-only teacher's own average move sits essentially at the middle — seven of its eight steps are random — yet the learner it produces queries far from the middle, and works. The last-seven-steps teacher's average move sits far from the middle, yet the learner it produces sits at the middle, and fails.

So the teacher's average behaviour predicts the learner's backwards. Only the first step predicts it forwards. That is the answer to this whole question, measured directly rather than inferred from which runs happened to win.

The failing runs do not perform badly — they do nothing at all

Every run starts from an initial set of points chosen before the method takes over. Comparing each run's best result against its own starting set:

All four failing variants improve on their own starting points in zero of five runs. The final best value equals the starting best value exactly, on every seed — twenty runs, not one improvement. The control, the first-step-only teacher and the best variant improve in five of five.

This also explains a number that has recurred throughout this investigation. Four different failing variants all score exactly 43.94, and it was never clear why they should tie so precisely. 43.94 is the starting set. They coincide because none of them contributes anything, so all that is left is the design they were handed.

And the middle of the box is genuinely a poor place to be: the best value reachable anywhere within a short radius of the centre is 85.8, against 273.0 for the box as a whole. A run confined there cannot beat its own starting points — which is precisely what the zero-of-five result shows.

What this does not settle

Drifting inward is now shown to be harmful, not merely a symptom — but what makes a policy drift inward is still open. Nothing here intervenes to prevent the drift and observe the result. The failing side is also thin: four variants out of 28.

It does not explain why the two benchmarks differ, and a second attempt at that failed this week too. The idea was that on one benchmark good points simply lie further from the middle, making escape most of the problem. Sampling the two objectives directly refuted it — the effect is fourteen times stronger on the benchmark where the explanation needed it to be weaker. That gap stays open.

One earlier idea was dropped here on evidence: that the drift compounds through the teacher. It does not — every teacher's own moves spread outward over a run, including in the runs that fail.

The learner memorises one point per stepnew

This is the mechanism, and it can be checked with an identity rather than an argument. A model that simply predicts the average of what it is shown has an error exactly equal to the spread of what it is shown. Both quantities were already being recorded. If the ratio is one, the model is doing nothing but averaging.

Every variant sits at the averaging value, across a 2.5× range of teacher spread. The learner accounts for between nothing and a fifth of what its teacher does — and on three of the five, nothing at all.

The fraction it does account for is not noise: it tracks how many steps each route has. With one-step routes there is only one position to learn, so a point-per-step model can explain nothing beyond it — and it explains exactly 0.0%. The share rises with route length, reaching a fifth for the variant whose first step differs most sharply from the rest. That is the signature of a model learning one point per step, and nothing within a step.

Learning more does not help. It is where the point lands that matters.

Read the first and last rows together. The variant that accounts for the most of its teacher — a fifth — is the worst performer in the study. The variant that accounts for nothing at all is the best. Since only the first step is ever consulted at decision time, what determines the outcome is where that single memorised point sits, not how much of the teacher was absorbed.

This also resolves something that sat unexplained for weeks: the failing variants fit their teachers better as their runs proceed. In the worst variant the error falls by a fifth while its queries close to a third of their starting distance from the middle — and its teacher's own spread does not change at all. It is fitting better by averaging harder, and the average sits in the middle of the box.

It holds on the second benchmark too — and a second check appeared unplannednew

I had recorded, twice, that this comparison could only be made on one benchmark because the two quantities were rarely logged. That was wrong — they are recorded on eighteen runs across both. The test was available the whole time.

Across ten variants on both benchmarks, the ratio runs from 0.750 to 1.054. Every one sits at the averaging value. The finding is not an artefact of a single problem.

And a second check fell out that was never designed. The theory forces the “explains nothing” result in two distinct situations: when routes are one step long, and when a teacher behaves identically at every step, because then there is only one position to learn either way. The one-step case was the original prediction and three independent variants confirm it. The second case is the random teacher on eight-step routes — a completely different route to the same forced answer — and it reads 0.0%.

Stated as a rule: the learner accounts for some of its teacher only when that teacher behaves differently at different steps. All ten variants fit.

What this still rests on

The comparison is about training; the fact that only the first step reaches the decision comes from separate measurements, not this table. Ratios slightly above one are expected, since the averaging benchmark is computed on the same data it is compared against. Five seeds per variant.

Why a perfect teacher cannot helpthe answer

Everything above composes into one sentence. The learner reproduces the average of its teacher's first move — and on the main benchmark that average is worse than simply running the teacher. Averaging over many routes is real work, but it is all the learner does, and here it costs rather than pays.

Now consider what a perfect teacher actually looks like. Its routes end at the optimum. But they start wherever the route happens to start. All the quality is in the destination; the first steps are close to arbitrary. So the average of those first moves is the middle of the box — which is exactly where the failing runs end up, and why they never beat the points they were handed at the start.

The quality was never transmitted, because only the first step is ever read and the quality lives in the destination.

The account predicts the learner's output, not just explains it

The teacher's average move was already being recorded — averaged across all steps of a route. That gives the test its own control. When routes are one step long, that recorded average is the first-move average, so it should predict where the learner queries. When routes are eight steps long and the first step differs sharply from the rest, it should not.

Short routes: the prediction lands within 0.11–0.16. Long split routes: it misses by 0.62–0.71. For scale, changing only the random seed moves the query about 0.82. That is a 4.5–5.3× gap between the cases where the account says it should work and the cases where it says it should fail — and which case a variant falls into was fixed by its route length long before these numbers existed.

And now it has been tested by intervention, not just observationnew

Everything above is a correlation: the learner's chosen point tracks its teacher's average first move. None of it moved that average on purpose to see whether the learner followed. So I did.

The teacher's first move was translated a fixed distance toward the middle of the box — that step only, nothing else touched. The possible outcomes were written down first, including the one that would have shown the whole account was wrong.

The teacher's average moved 0.419. The learner's own chosen point moved 0.459. It reproduced the imposed shift essentially one-for-one. The bar for calling the account confirmed was that the learner reproduce at least half of it.

The cost of that shift shows what is at stake in where the point lands: the error went from 13.69 to 43.18, and the run's ability to improve on the points it started with collapsed from five of five to one of five. Pushed to the middle of the box, the method stops contributing almost entirely.

One honesty note on that second half: the shift was deliberately aimed at the middle, which earlier work had already identified as a bad place to be. So the error result tests two things at once and does not independently establish the second. The one-for-one tracking is unaffected — it measures only whether the learner follows.

It also explains why nothing tried on the teacher side ever mattered. Six explanations were tested and all six changed how the teacher decides. But the decision rule never reaches the output — different competent rules share almost the same first-move average. The only two changes that ever helped worked differently: one selected which routes to keep, the other made routes shorter. Neither changed the rule; both changed what was being averaged.

The test came back against the methodcorrected

An earlier section of this report said the learner's averaging was "about as good as running the teacher". That rested on an older experiment on a different benchmark, scored on a different measure, with the two sides spending their budgets differently. It found the learner ahead on seven of ten runs and called it unresolved — which it was.

The like-for-like replacement has now run: the same teacher, no learner at all, on the main benchmark, on the frozen measure. Its possible outcomes were written down first, including the one worst for this project. That is the one that came back.

Removing the learner entirely improves the result on every single run — 12.97 against 15.82, better on five of five seeds. The pre-registered bar for calling it a real difference was 1.72; the gap is 2.85.

But that is a statement about the default setup, and the picture flips for the configurations this project actually advocates. With its region-constraint option switched on, the method scores 9.81–11.59 and beats the same teacher-only arm — the best configuration by 3.15 points on four of five seeds, close to a mirror image of the default's loss. The catch is that this is not a fair fight in the other direction: the region constraint is a training-time mechanism, so the teacher-only arm never receives it, and its candidate pool stays unconstrained. Whether a teacher given the same constraint would win again is untested, and not something to assert.

So the honest headline has three parts that must travel together: on this benchmark's default path the learner costs 2.85 points against its own teacher; the configurations that beat that teacher do so with a mechanism the teacher was not given; and on the other benchmark the whole comparison reverses. Any one of these reported alone misleads.

The obvious objection is that the teacher spends its budget differently — more of it on cheap evaluations. That is ruled out by an experiment already run for another purpose: forcing the learner to spend 6.4× more on cheap evaluations moved its score by 0.06. The teacher's advantage is not about where it spends.

One qualifier, so this is not overstated in the other direction: the teacher chooses by searching over two hundred candidate points, while the learner emits a single point directly. That asymmetry is the comparison — it is what replacing search with a learned policy means — but the honest phrasing is that the learner is a net negative against a rule that performs explicit search.

The older experiment's opposite finding on the second benchmark has now been checked properly — and it was right. See below.

On the second benchmark the answer reverses — by a wider marginnew

The same test, the same code, the second benchmark. The result is the opposite, and it is not close.

There the learner beats the teacher on all five runs, by 13.89 points — 7.99 against 21.88 — where on the first benchmark it lost on all five runs by 2.85. Two benchmarks, opposite answers, each unanimous, and the win is roughly five times the size of the loss.

So the honest statement is not "the learner helps" or "the learner hurts". It is that the value of what the learner does depends on the problem, and reverses. The description of what it does — memorising one point per step, using only the first — is unchanged by this. What changes is what that is worth.

The older experiment that first suggested this is now vindicated rather than explained away. I had written down, before running, the outcome that would have let me attribute its finding to a weaker measure. That is not what came back.

Why the reversal? The two benchmarks price their expensive evaluations very differently — one at eight times the cheap one, the other at two. On the expensive-ratio problem the teacher's choice between cheap and expensive is erratic across runs, swinging from never using the cheap source to using it 99% of the time, while the learner stays steady. Steadiness should be worth more where mistakes cost more. That is a hypothesis, and five runs cannot support it — it is written down as the next thing to test, not as the explanation.

Three explanations, retractedretracted

Each of these was reported before it was tested properly. Each is withdrawn, with the experiment that killed it.

“The method's own rule is what the learner needs”

retracted by the UCB teacher — 15.13 against the control's 15.82, improving 5/5

A completely different, standard selection rule matches the control on regret, on improvement rate, and on the training signal. It is not the rule.

“The failing teachers generate too weak a signal to aim at”

retracted by the frozen control — signal 0.32, indistinguishable from the failing teachers, and it works anyway

This was my leading account for four rounds. The frozen control generates a signal as weak as the failing teachers and performs like the working ones. A weak signal does not cause the failure. The measurement behind the account was right; the causal story was not.

“It's about whether the teacher can adapt”

retracted by measurement — freezing costs ~9% of the signal; the failing teachers lose ~70%

This one was itself an over-correction: I found a genuinely uncontrolled variable and promoted it to the explanation without checking whether it was large enough to do the work. It was not.

The current accountcausal test pending

What the three working teachers share is that they query places the model itself picked — including the frozen one, whose fixed route was recorded from the model earlier. The three failing teachers query places chosen by something outside the model: the true optimum, a draw from the true objective, or nothing at all.

An action chosen by something the learner cannot observe cannot be learned from what it observes. The prediction is that the network gives up and predicts the average — and that is visible in its own behaviour.

This also resolves something that sat unexplained for weeks. The network fits the failing teachers better — lower training loss — while emitting worse points. Low loss on clustered targets means it is predicting their average, not learning a mapping.

Since this was written — and now settled

The reasoning above was wrong in an instructive way. It assumed the learner cannot fit the failing teachers. It fits them fine — two and a half to four times better than the best possible constant guess, on all three. The failure is not in the learning.

What replaced it. The learner is only ever asked for a decision at the very first step of a route, and at that first step the situations it trained on are nearly identical to one another. So it can only reproduce its teacher's average first move. Teachers whose first move is chosen by consulting the model hand it a useful point. Teachers whose first move is a random draw hand it the average of a random draw — the middle of the search space, and useless.

And this was then tested by changing something, not by fitting more numbers. Two teachers were built: one consults the model only on its first step and moves at random for the other seven; the other does the reverse. The second follows the model on seven steps out of eight and is the better teacher by any conventional measure.

What this settles, and what it costs

A bad first step is fatal, whatever the rest of the route does. The seven-step teacher fails completely on both benchmarks tested — 43.94 against a working 15.82 on one, 46.45 against 7.99 on the other — and its learner queries the dead centre of the search space in both. That is the part that replicates.

Whether the other seven steps matter at all depends on the problem, and I got this wrong at first. On the first benchmark the one-step teacher matched the method itself (16.96 against 15.82), so those seven steps contributed essentially nothing — they affected only the bill, taking 1.82× the wall-clock side by side on the same machine for a worse result. On the second benchmark the one-step teacher is worse than the method on every seed (25.16 against 7.99). There, the later steps do real work.

I reported the stronger claim — "only the first step matters" — after four of five runs had finished on the second benchmark. The fifth reversed it. The claim that survives on both is the weaker one above.

That still explains the original puzzle. How good a route is, and how varied, both describe where the route goes — while the first step is what the learner is actually asked to reproduce. A perfect route's first step is a random starting point, whose average is the middle of the space, and that is exactly where its learner ends up querying.

Still open. The learner's query lands near its teacher's average first move, not exactly on it — about five standard errors away, on both benchmarks. Something else is also operating, and it has not been identified.

The project's best result survives the cut, at about a fifth of the time. That is the practical payoff: the same answer for a fraction of the compute.

Two honest limits. The apparent improvement from shortening is not real at five seeds — the run-to-run spread is larger than the difference. What the data supports is "no worse, much faster", and I initially wrote "better" before checking.

And shortening costs consistency. The best setting at full length is by far the steadiest configuration measured, varying by 0.41 across seeds against 2.79 when shortened. If predictability matters more than wall-clock, the long version is still the one to use — and nothing in the average regret shows that.

Honest limits

The dispersion evidence is behavioural, measured at five seeds per arm. The same class of evidence has already produced one published finding that was later retracted, and one registered prediction that came out backwards. It is a consistent correlate across two benchmarks and seven teachers — not a demonstrated cause.

A precision claim made earlier here has been withdrawn: a standalone reconstruction of the training signal was reported as matching the measured values to within 8%, but it is itself noisy to about 6%, so that agreement was barely distinguishable from noise. It reproduces the separation between working and failing teachers — a factor of three — reliably. It does not reproduce the individual values, and on the one case it had never been checked against, it was wrong by a factor of nearly three.
